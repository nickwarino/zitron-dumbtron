What Top-Tier Analysts Say About AI

A verified database of 110 claims from seven serious thinkers about AI, and a lit review of why they reach the opposite of Zitron's conclusion. Every source was checked live; every quote is verbatim.

Lit review: the same worries, a different verdict

The striking thing about reading the serious literature on AI next to Ed Zitron is how much they share. Levine dissects the circular financing. Tooze flags a leveraged bet on disruption. DeLong says lab profits may be elusive. Mazzucato names the extraction and the concentration. Acemoglu warns about excessive automation. Perez expects a crash. Bruenig grants the billionaire wealth and the labor displacement. Every concern Zitron raises, someone here raises too, often with more rigor and better sourcing.

And not one of them concludes that AI is a fraud. That is the puzzle worth explaining: the same inputs, the opposite output. The reason is structural. The panel keeps three questions apart, is the technology real, is it overvalued, who captures its gains, and answers each on its own evidence. Zitron fuses them, and lets the answer to the second and third decide the first. If the financing is circular and the gains are captured and the labs lose money, then, for Zitron, the technology must be fake. For the panel, a real technology can be overvalued, badly governed, and unprofitable all at once, and usually is, early on.

Individually, they emphasize different things, none of them fakeness

Matt Levine stays in the finance. He explains, better than anyone, why the Nvidia-OpenAI-AMD-Oracle deals are structurally circular, and then refuses the leap to fraud: the structure is strange, the question is whether the future revenues show up, not whether the thing is a con. Adam Tooze works the macro: AI capex as a de facto stimulus carrying US growth, a K-shaped economy, and the cleanest taxonomy of the bubble, metaverse versus railways versus airlines, where only the first matches Zitron's read. Carlota Perez supplies the history: every technological revolution installs itself through a financial mania that crashes, and the crash is the gate to a deployment golden age, not proof the technology was hollow.

Brad DeLong splits the welfare from the returns: natural-language interfaces are "absolutely wonderful" and the consumer surplus is enormous, even as the same competition that delivers it starves the labs of profit. Users win, investors may not, and neither fact makes the technology fake. Daron Acemoglu, the most skeptical of the productivity hype, still states it flatly: "I am not an AI denier." His problem is the direction of the technology and the institutions around it, augmentation versus excessive automation, not whether it works. Mariana Mazzucato follows the public money: AI's foundations were publicly funded, the gains are being captured as "algorithmic rents" by a few incumbents, and the failure is governance and direction, which only makes sense if the technology is real and worth capturing. Matt Bruenig, the socialist, calls technology skepticism "the most indefensible type," uses LLMs daily, builds labor-law tools for unions with them, and routes the entire critique through ownership and the welfare state: "a great case for socialism, but not really a case against any particular labor-saving technology."

Taken together, the disagreement with Zitron is not about the facts

Line up the seven and the pattern is unmistakable. They span the spectrum, a Nobel institutionalist, a finance columnist, a socialist labor lawyer, a mission-economy reformer, the historian of technological revolutions, two economic historians, and they converge on a frame Zitron never adopts: the technology is real, the serious questions are political-economic, and the answers are about ownership, direction, distribution, and governance. The bubble may pop, the labs may never profit, the gains may be captured by a few, and through all of it the tool remains a real, useful, transformative technology.

That is the difference between a critique and a dismissal. The panel has a positive theory of what is wrong, shareholder primacy, monopoly, the wrong direction of innovation, a missing welfare floor, and a positive program, public ownership, a social wealth fund, antitrust, the welfare state, public direction of the technology's path. Zitron has the negative only: it is fake and it will collapse. When you already believe the gains are not real, you have nothing to redistribute and nothing to govern, which is why the fraud thesis, however energizing, is also a dead end for the politics it claims to serve. The constructive left position, the People's Policy Project and Matt Bruenig and the case that the state can invent the goods, starts from the opposite premise: the technology is the wonder workers made, and the fight is over who owns it.
