Zitron Dumbtron

Every dated AI claim Ed Zitron has made, pulled from his full archive and scored two ways: how right it is, and how much rides on it.

This audit reads all 110 of Ed Zitron's AI posts since 2020, scores 610 dated claims against the record as of June 2026, and stress-tests six of his core arguments. It is not a dunk and not a defense. It weights each claim by what rides on it. Being right that a company loses money is not the same as being right that the technology is a fraud.

"It might be a bubble" is table stakes. Everyone says it, including Altman. The question is the rest.

Two axes run through the audit. Correctness, one to five, from a sourced verdict on each claim. Stakes, S1 to S7, how much rides on the claim being true, from whether the tech is real and improving (S1) down to a pricing tweak (S7). For Zitron the two axes point in opposite directions.

The Rating

Ed Zitron is one of the most prominent AI critics writing, a confident truth-teller with a large audience, most recently making his case on Chris Hayes' podcast (click for the annotated transcript). He pitches himself less as a careful analyst than a brave one. But the volume and the certainty stake a claim to the authority of a Matt Levine or an Adam Tooze. Hold him to that bar. Two skills, two grades.

As a finance and company reporter, B. The scoops on token billing and inference costs are real, unprofitability is his best-supported theme, and on company and sector finance only 21 percent of his scored claims are wrong.

As an analyst, D. An analyst's job is the foundational call. On the two questions that matter most, is the tech real and does it deliver value, he is more wrong than right: across about 136 capability-and-utility claims, 81 wrong, 43 right. His distinctive version, useless, a con, hit a wall, not getting cheaper, is the wrong half. And he does not update. He made the crypto comparison in 2023 and still makes it in 2026, after agentic coding shipped.

What the foundational errors mean

Claims are not independent points to average. They are marshaled toward a conclusion. The accurate finance claims, OpenAI loses money, the financing is circular, are bricks in an argument whose foundation is that the technology is fake and finished. If the foundation is more wrong than right, the argument fails, however good the bricks. "Amazon loses money" was true every year of the late 1990s and useless to anyone arguing the internet was a fraud. An analysis is only as good as its load-bearing claim. His load-bearing claim is the one he gets wrong.

Four of six core arguments below land at "largely undercuts Zitron," two at "genuinely mixed," one at "partly supports." His distinctive claims score 3.04 out of 5; the consensus claims everyone makes score 3.75. He was more right about crypto (3.73) than about AI (3.09).

The Tell: He Called It Crypto

In 2023 his core move was to file generative AI next to crypto, NFTs, and the metaverse: hype with no product. That bet has a clean test, and four years has run it. Crypto and NFTs never produced durable mass use. Generative AI did. By 2026 ChatGPT has roughly 900 million weekly users, capability keeps climbing through reasoning and long tasks, a fixed level of capability costs about a thousandth what it did, and agentic coding through Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor is a real change in how software gets written. On the axis that separates a fraud from a technology, durable and growing use, the analogy has broken.

So the loud claims get marked down over time, not up. The crypto comparison was a defensible if aggressive prior in 2023. Repeating useless, a con, nobody wants it in 2026 is a failure to update. The hype was not all lies. Its core claim, that this is a transformative technology, especially for code, has largely held, in a way crypto's never did.

Nobody accidentally spends 500 million dollars a month on a tool nobody wants.

The sharpest evidence against him is his own. The scoops on how much companies burn on Claude Code and Codex document the demand he says does not exist. His numbers refute his thesis. This does not settle the bubble, still live, or ROI, still thin. It settles the claim he is most known for, against him.

The Right/Wrong Index

One score per AI claim, ranking every call he has made about AI from most wrong to most right. It folds in the three things that should decide how much a call counts: how accurate it was, how much was at stake, and how original it was. Being right about a thing everyone says is cheap. Being wrong about the load-bearing question is expensive. The index prices both.

How the score works

Accuracy sets the sign, from Correct at plus two through Mixed at zero to Wrong at minus two. Two multipliers scale the size. Stakes run from S1 foundational at full weight down to S7 operational at a third. Originality gives a distinctive claim full weight and a table-stakes claim everyone makes a little over half. The product runs from minus 100, a flat-wrong call on a high-stakes, original question, to plus 100, a dead-right one. Mixed and table-stakes claims fall toward the middle by design. Unverifiable and not-yet-resolved claims carry no score.

Six Arguments, Stress-Tested

His core moves, each researched and then handed to an adversarial reviewer told to attack both the research and Zitron. The verdict is the reviewer's revised call.

Is AI Getting Cheaper?

He says no, flatly and often. In his June 2026 piece AI Doesn't Have ROI: "AI is more expensive today than it was three years ago, and it is not getting cheaper. Sam Altman's comments about 'intelligence too cheap to meter' were lies." He treats "the cost of inference is coming down" as a booster lie. "Cheaper" has several meanings, so test each. The most telling is the cost of a fixed capability over time, a GPT-4-level model, because it holds quality constant while price moves.

The Measured Counterweight: Benedict Evans

Benedict Evans is the closest thing to the Levine-and-Tooze read the rating invokes: a measured analyst working from primary data. His May 2026 deck, AI Eats the World, lands where this audit lands. Capability, cost, and demand are real and fast. The business model is unsettled. The deck is below, then the slides that bear on Zitron.

Inference efficiency is growing 50 to 100x per year
Slide 20 · "Price, use and capex are out of equilibrium"

Evans models the sector as explosive demand growth, times inference efficiency rising 50 to 100x a year, times an open question on model efficiency and asset life, times a new frontier model every 6 to 9 months.

Undermines Zitron  This demolishes his claim that AI is not getting cheaper. A neutral analyst puts the efficiency gain at 50 to 100x a year, matching the Epoch and a16z figures. It also validates the one real nuance: usage can outrun even that, so a user's bill still rises.

The S-Tier Benchmark: Matt Bruenig

The scale tops out at Bruenig-level cohesion and logic, so it is worth showing what that looks like here. In January 2026 Matt Bruenig, who runs the People's Policy Project, advises Bernie Sanders and Zohran Mamdani, and practices union-side labor law, wrote Some Thoughts on AI. He is a socialist, not a tech booster, Zitron's political tribe. He reaches the opposite conclusion, for one reason: he keeps the layers separate.

Bruenig splits AI skepticism into three independent kinds and grades each on its own.

The technology itself

"The most indefensible type." If it works it proves itself through use, and it already is. The error-rate objection misses that professional use supplies context, so the error-prone experience is the context-free chatbot most casual users see. He is a heavy user: Claude Code turns an hour of statistical programming into seconds, it built the scrapers and database behind his 13,000-subscriber labor-law newsletter, and he does legal research with retrieval and map-reduce.

The valuation

The bubble question. "Reasonable, but not that interesting and hard to really know." Overvaluation happens all the time. This is the table-stakes layer, conceded and set aside, the same move this audit makes.

The distribution

The real concern: labor reallocation and unemployment spells, billionaires minted, rich industrialists distorting politics. And here is the line Zitron never draws. These are, in Bruenig's words, "valid critiques of the capitalist system, not of LLMs. It's a great case for socialism, but not really a case against any particular labor-saving technology." The problem is "with capitalism not with economic production and innovation per se."

He holds capability, valuation, and distribution as separate questions and answers each: the tech is real, the valuation may be inflated, the distributive harm is capitalism's, not the technology's. Zitron fuses all three into one verdict, that the tech is a con. The fusion is the error.

This is not pro-AI or pro-capitalist bias. A man who writes policy for Bernie Sanders, whose Social Wealth Fund proposal argues at length that capitalism distributes the gains of innovation indefensibly, looks at the same political economy and concludes the technology is good and the fight is over who owns its gains. He names the irony: the left's anti-AI reflex is partly anti-tech-sector animus, yet tech is the sector AI most disrupts. "If you don't like the tech sector, a new technology that makes coding trivially easy seems like exactly the thing you might invent to stick it to them."

A Sanders policy adviser looked at AI and said the tech is real and the enemy is how its gains are distributed. That is S-tier, and it is the opposite of calling the tech a con.

The point generalizes. The economy is broken for institutional reasons, ownership and distribution, not because the technology is fake. That is why the rot-economy verdict here is "right that something is broken, fuzzy on the mechanism." The serious left puts the problem in who captures the gains, a structural question. His answer is concrete: a social wealth fund that hands every American a share and an annual dividend, the proposal that inspired Bernie's AI sovereign-wealth-fund bill. Zitron puts it in the technology and the vibe of growth, the wrong target, and one a socialist economist who uses the tools declines to aim at.

He put it more plainly still on his podcast in February 2026 (click for the annotated transcript). AI is just labor-saving technology, filed with word processing, the assembly line, and fertilizer, not with crypto. Two million Americans lose a job every month already, so the harm is the missing welfare floor, not the tool. And the tool is "the wonder we have made," built by the workers who wrote the Transformer paper, not by Altman. If five people capture trillions from software they did not make, "that's your system, dude," not the technology. The fight is ownership. The technology is real.

He does not just argue it. He builds with it, for workers.

Bruenig used an LLM to write a technical book on NLRB law, and he runs NLRB Research, whose AI assistant is a Claude skill grounded in 365,000-plus primary sources, NLRB and MSPB decisions, LMRDA filings, collective bargaining agreements, and California PERB rulings, that turns a labor-law question into a cited legal memo. It is built for unions and workers, and it makes labor-law research that used to take an expensive firm cheap, fast, and accessible. That is AI tilting power toward labor, not capital.

That one product undercuts him in five concrete ways. It is a real, paid, used tool, not vaporware, which cuts against his recurring claim that the demand is not real and never arriving. It is grounded and cited against primary law, which is how the hallucination problem he calls fatal is actually managed in serious professional work. It does genuinely useful legal drafting and research, against his line that AI writing is soulless. It answers what the tool does today, the present-tense test he sets for boosters. And it arms unions and workers against employers who can afford the big firms, the opposite of his framing that it is a con. The same capability he calls a fraud is, in a labor lawyer's hands, a lever for the working class. The fight is ownership, and here the left is already winning a piece of it.

The Real Fight: Ownership, Not Fakeness

If the diagnosis is distribution under capitalism, what is the program? This is where the serious left analysis leaves Zitron furthest behind, because it has one and he does not.

The labor shock is real, and the fix is boring

In a March 2026 piece Bruenig argues the distributive damage of a labor-saving technology is the most solved problem in policy. Separate the direct effects, like AI cheating tools or companion apps that displace real connection, which are unpredictable and need specific fixes, from the distributive effects, who gets the income, which are well understood. Polling agrees: a Blue Rose survey found 54 percent want AI unemployment met with good jobs and 49 percent want a tax on AI profiteers to fund them. No exotic framework is needed. Ordinary social democracy does it: income support, taxing the winners, full employment. Bruenig has held this line for years; in Jacobin in April 2023 he argued the fix for AI job displacement is a robust welfare state, since labor reallocation is only a disaster without egalitarian institutions. The panic that AI demands brand-new policy is, in his words, emblematic of an impoverished policymaking environment.

The deeper answer is public ownership of innovation

Nick Warino's report for the People's Policy Project, The Nordic Model Invents the Goods, documents that the Nordic states sit at the technological frontier through state-owned firms, strong unions, and universal welfare, which disproves the claim that the public sector stifles innovation. Its sharper point is that equality and innovation are complementary, not in tension: sectoral bargaining compresses wages across the economy, which both reduces inequality and forces more creative destruction, because firms that cannot compete on cheap labor have to innovate instead. The American problem is not a failure to invent. It is that the gains flow to the rich. The question AI raises is not whether the technology is real but who owns it and who captures its returns.

And here is the inversion Zitron gets backwards

The doomer instinct, shared by the Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez data-center moratorium, is to stop AI: pause the build, choke the data centers. In a June 2026 exchange with Matt Stoller, Bruenig points out that a pause is exactly what the incumbents want. A development pause lets them book counterfactual R&D as profit. A data-center pause lets them avoid the capex and charge more for a constrained token supply. The cartel move is to slow down. "The best way to stick it to them is to push them forward and socialize the equity." Use the competition that is forcing the labs to burn every dollar into capex, then publicly own the result.

That reframes the fact Zitron leans on hardest. His Hayes line, "none of these businesses are profitable, not a single one of them," reads to him as proof the product is worthless. Read through the political economy, the unprofitability is competition forcing the labs to plow everything into building, which is the opposite of a monopoly extracting rent, and exactly what a public stake would capture. It is why Sanders' other bill, the American AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act, takes equity rather than banning the build: a public fund, board seats, and a dividend to ordinary Americans.

So the serious left ends up at the opposite of Zitron on every axis. The tech is real, not a con. The unprofitability is reinvestment, not worthlessness. The danger is distribution, not the technology. And the strategy is build and socialize, not pause and moralize.

A pause is the cartel's move. The way to beat the AI oligarchs is to push the technology forward and take the equity.

This is the throughline of the audit in political-economy terms. Zitron draws real energy from real problems, the power of the labs, the treatment of workers, the founders' arrogance, then aims it at the technology and predicts collapse. Bruenig and the People's Policy Project share the politics, keep the layers separate, and aim the same energy at ownership and distribution, the target that can be hit. His verdict that the technology is a fraud bound to collapse is not just wrong on the facts. It points the fight in the one direction that helps the people he wants to beat.

Reliability and the Human Baseline

Hallucination is Zitron's main reliability argument, and it deserves the deepest look, because the strongest case against him is the one he half-relies on. "Is hallucination inevitable" is two questions conflated. The worst-case theory papers (Xu et al., Banerjee et al.) prove by diagonalization that no computable model can be correct on every computable function, so a fully general problem-solver must hallucinate somewhere. But those authors concede the proofs say nothing about the rate on real tasks. The practical literature, including OpenAI's own Why Language Models Hallucinate, Suzuki et al., and the Oracle Escape result, argues the rate is a fixable incentive and grounding problem. "Inevitable in the worst case" and "negligible in the average case" are both true. Zitron leans on the first to imply the second is impossible. That is the equivocation, and the authority he would most want, OpenAI's paper, is a reducible argument he would have to misread to cite.

Reliability in 2026 is bimodal, and that is the fact his framing flattens. Unsupervised on the open web it is genuinely bad: the CJR Tow Center found eight AI search engines wrong on more than 60 percent of news-citation queries, the BBC and EBU found 45 percent of responses had a significant issue, and Stanford's RegLab found commercial legal AI hallucinating 17 to 33 percent despite "hallucination-free" marketing, with 700-plus court cases of fabricated citations now on record. Grounded and verifiable, it is genuinely good: SimpleQA accuracy jumps from under 40 percent to over 90 percent with tools, and the best grounded systems sit under 1.5 percent hallucination. "Unreliable" is true for one mode and false for the other. He treats the bad mode as the whole.

The right standard is comparative, not perfect: better than the human otherwise used, faster and cheaper. The human gold standard was always a myth. Loftus showed human memory confabulates confidently, so non-fabrication was never the bar for people. AI is held to a superhuman one: in a radiology study people tolerated an 11.3 percent error rate from a human and 6.8 percent from the machine. Karpathy sharpens it: generation is the mechanism, the model is always dreaming, so the fix is grounding and system design, not a perfect oracle. Willison adds that a code hallucination is the least dangerous kind, because the code runs and the error surfaces. The augmentation evidence closes it: the BCG-Harvard experiment found 40 percent higher quality inside the frontier, and the support-agent trial 14 percent overall, 34 percent for novices. A technology with those effects is not useless.

But the human analogy has a real limit, and it is exactly where Zitron is strongest, so honesty requires stating it. AI error is shaped differently from human error. It arrives in confident, fluent prose with no tells, where a person would hedge. It is systematic and correlated across millions of identical outputs, so one failure mode propagates at scale instead of being idiosyncratic and self-limiting. And it is indifferent to truth rather than mistaken about it. Add automation bias, where humans rubber-stamp the machine, deskilling, where a clinician's unassisted detection rate fell after AI exposure, and the accountability gap, and "human in the loop" is often a fiction that launders machine error. On the unsupervised high-stakes case, legal citations, medical recall, news, he wins cleanly, and vendor promises that the problem is solved are false.

Reliability is also jagged. AI improves fastest where output is verifiable, code and math, because a checkable reward trains it, and lags where quality is subjective, writing and taste, where no such signal exists. So the soulless-slop charge has more standing for prose than for code, and the cannot-really-code charge has less. The map is not useless and not magic but a jagged frontier.

Hallucination is real, bimodal, and not going to zero. But "it hallucinates, therefore it is useless" is a category error the very literature he invokes refutes.

Does this move the rating? No. It makes his dismissal look worse, because his strongest-sounding move, citing inevitability, is an equivocation, and the comparative standard that governs deployment is one he never applies. But it also hands him his best ammunition, the error-shape disanalogy, which is real. A D-tier analyst loud about a genuine problem is still pointing at one. His D is not for being wrong that hallucination exists. It is for the method: selecting worst cases, misreading his own sources, freezing a moving target. The topic is legitimate. The reasoning fails.

His mix of claims by year, as a share of each year's output. The drift is real: from broad political-economy and tech-skepticism toward company and sector finance, where his reporting is strongest.
