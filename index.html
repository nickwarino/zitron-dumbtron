Zitron Dumbtron

Every dated AI claim Ed Zitron has made, pulled from his full archive and scored two ways: how right it is, and how much rides on it.

A swarm of agents read all 110 of Zitron's AI-relevant posts back to 2020, pulled 610 dated claims, and scored each one against the record as of June 2026. Then six of his core arguments were researched and adversarially checked. The point is not to dunk or to defend. It is to weight his claims by what actually rides on them, because being right that a company loses money is not the same as being right that the technology is a fraud.

It might be a bubble is table stakes. Everyone says it, including Altman. The question is the rest of what he says.

Two axes run through everything below. Correctness, one to five, derived from a sourced verdict on each claim. And stakes, S1 to S7, how consequential the claim is, from the foundational question of whether the tech is real and improving (S1) down to a pricing tweak or a single fact (S7). The headline finding is that these two axes point in opposite directions for him.

The Rating

He sells himself as the definitive analyst of the AI bubble, in the company of Matt Levine and Adam Tooze. Grade him there, not against the average poster. Two skills, two grades.

As a finance and company reporter, B. His scoops on token billing and inference costs are real, unprofitability is his best-supported theme, and on company and sector finance only about 21 percent of his scored claims are wrong.

As an analyst, D. The job of an analyst is the foundational call, and on the two questions that matter most, is the technology real and does it deliver value, he is more wrong than right. Across about 140 capability-and-utility claims, 69 are wrong and 53 are right. His distinctive version of them, useless, a con, hit a wall, not getting cheaper, is the wrong half, and he is not updating. He made the crypto comparison in 2023 and is still making it in 2026, after agentic coding shipped.

What the foundational errors mean

Claims are not independent points to be averaged. They are marshaled toward a conclusion. His accurate finance claims, that OpenAI loses money and the financing is circular, are bricks in an argument whose foundation is the claim that the technology is fake and finished. If the foundation is a coin flip, the building does not stand, however well the bricks are laid. "Amazon loses money" was true every year of the late 1990s and useless to anyone who used it to argue the internet was a fraud. An analysis is only as good as its load-bearing claim, and the load-bearing claim is the one he gets wrong.

Four of six core arguments below land at "largely undercuts Zitron," two at "genuinely mixed," one at "partly supports." His distinctive claims score 3.27 out of 5; the consensus claims everyone makes score 3.88. He was more right about crypto (3.73) than about AI (3.32).

The Tell: He Called It Crypto

His core move in 2023 was to file generative AI next to crypto, NFTs, and the metaverse. Hype with no product underneath. That bet has a clean test, and four years has run it. Crypto and NFTs never produced durable mass use. Generative AI did. By 2026 ChatGPT has roughly 900 million weekly users, capability has climbed through reasoning and long-horizon tasks, the cost of a fixed level of capability has fallen about a thousandfold, and agentic coding through Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor is a real change in how software gets written. On the one axis that separates a fraud from a technology, durable and growing use, the analogy he built his brand on has broken.

So his loudest claims should be marked down over time, not up. The crypto comparison was a defensible if aggressive prior in 2023. Repeating useless, a con, nobody wants it in 2026, after the agentic shift, is a failure to update, which for an analyst is the cardinal sin. The hype he mocked was not all lies. Its central claim, that this is a genuinely transformative technology, especially for code, has been substantially borne out, in a way crypto's never was.

You do not accidentally spend 500 million dollars a month on a tool nobody wants.

The sharpest evidence against him is his own. His best reporting, the scoops on how much companies burn on Claude Code and Codex, documents exactly the demand he says does not exist. His numbers refute his thesis. None of this clears the bubble question, which is still live, or the ROI question, which is still thin. But it settles the one he is most known for, and it settles it against him.

Six Arguments, Stress-Tested

His core moves, each researched and then handed to an adversarial reviewer told to attack both the research and Zitron. The verdict is the reviewer's revised call.

Is AI Getting Cheaper?

He says no, flatly and often. In his June 2026 piece AI Doesn't Have ROI: "AI is more expensive today than it was three years ago, and it is not getting cheaper. Sam Altman's comments about 'intelligence too cheap to meter' were lies." He treats "the cost of inference is coming down" as a booster lie. "Cheaper" has several meanings, so test each. The most telling is the cost of a fixed capability over time, a GPT-4-level model, because it holds quality constant while price moves.

The Measured Counterweight: Benedict Evans

If Zitron is the polemic, Benedict Evans is the closest thing to the Levine-and-Tooze read the rating measures Zitron against. A respected, non-hyperbolic analyst, his May 2026 deck "AI Eats the World" is built from primary data, and it lands almost exactly where this audit does. The capability, the cost curve, and the demand are real and fast. The business model is genuinely unsettled. Here is the full deck, then the slides that bear straight on Zitron's claims.

Undermines Zitron  This demolishes "AI is not getting cheaper." A neutral analyst puts the efficiency gain at 50 to 100x a year, matching the Epoch and a16z figures. It also validates the one real nuance: usage can outrun even that, so a user's bill still rises.

The S-Tier Benchmark: Matt Bruenig

The rating scale tops out at "Bruenig-level cohesion and logic," so it is worth showing what that looks like on this exact topic. In January 2026 Matt Bruenig, a socialist-left economist who runs the People's Policy Project, advises politicians like Bernie Sanders and Zohran Mamdani, and practices union-side labor law, wrote Some Thoughts on AI. He is not a tech booster. He is, if anything, Zitron's political tribe. And he reaches the opposite conclusion, for one reason: he refuses to let the layers contaminate each other.

Bruenig splits AI skepticism into three independent kinds and grades each on its own.

The technology itself

"The most indefensible type." If it works it proves itself through use, and it already is. The error-rate objection misses that professional use supplies context, so the error-prone experience is the context-free chatbot most casual users see. He is a heavy user: Claude Code turns an hour of statistical programming into seconds, it built the scrapers and database behind his 13,000-subscriber labor-law newsletter, and he does legal research with retrieval and map-reduce.

The valuation

The bubble question. "Reasonable, but not that interesting and hard to really know." Overvaluation happens all the time. This is the table-stakes layer, conceded and set aside, the same move this audit makes.

The distribution

The real concern: labor reallocation, unemployment spells, billionaires minted, rich industrialists distorting politics. And here is the line Zitron never draws. These are, in Bruenig's words, "valid critiques of the capitalist system, not of LLMs. It's a great case for socialism, but not really a case against any particular labor-saving technology." The problem is "with capitalism not with economic production and innovation per se."

That is the whole game. Bruenig holds capability, valuation, and distribution as separate questions and answers each honestly: the tech is real and useful, the valuation may be inflated, and the distributive harm is capitalism's fault, not the technology's. Zitron fuses all three into one verdict, that the tech is a con, and the fusion is the error.

The contrast kills the easy dismissal. You cannot wave this away as pro-AI or pro-capitalist bias. A man who writes policy for Bernie Sanders, who has argued at length that capitalism distributes the gains of innovation in an indefensible way, looks at the same political economy Zitron looks at and concludes that the technology is genuinely good and that the thing to fight is the ownership and distribution of its gains. He even names the irony: the left's anti-AI reflex is partly anti-tech-sector animus, yet the sector AI most disrupts is tech itself. "If you don't like the tech sector, a new technology that makes coding trivially easy seems like exactly the thing you might invent to stick it to them."

A Bernie Sanders policy adviser looked at AI and said the tech is real and the enemy is capitalism's distribution of its gains. That is what S-tier looks like, and it is the opposite of calling the tech a con.

This is also the rigorous version of a point worth making plainly: the economy is broken for institutional reasons, ownership and the distribution of gains, not because the technology is fake. Bruenig says exactly that, which is why the "rot economy" verdict on this page is "right that something is broken, fuzzy on the mechanism." The serious left analysis puts the problem in who captures the gains, a structural and institutional question. Zitron puts it in the technology and the vibe of growth, which is the wrong target, and a target a socialist economist who actually uses the tools declines to aim at.

The Real Fight: Ownership, Not Fakeness

If the diagnosis is distribution under capitalism, what is the program? This is where the serious left analysis leaves Zitron furthest behind, because it has one and he does not.

The labor shock is real, and the fix is boring

In a March 2026 piece Bruenig argues the distributive damage of a labor-saving technology is the most solved problem in policy. Separate the direct effects, like AI cheating tools or companion apps that displace real connection, which are unpredictable and need specific fixes, from the distributive effects, who gets the income, which are well understood. Polling bears it out: a Blue Rose survey found 54 percent want AI unemployment met with good jobs and 49 percent want a tax on AI profiteers to pay for it. You do not need an exotic new framework. You need ordinary social democracy: income support, taxing the winners, full employment. He has held this line for years: back in Jacobin in April 2023 he argued the solution to AI job displacement is a robust welfare state, because labor reallocation is only a disaster in a society without egalitarian institutions. The panic that AI demands brand-new policy is, in his words, emblematic of an impoverished policymaking environment.

The deeper answer is public ownership of innovation

Nick Warino's report for the People's Policy Project, The Nordic Model Invents the Goods, documents that the Nordic states sit at the technological frontier through state-owned firms, strong unions, and universal welfare, which disproves the claim that the public sector stifles innovation. Its sharper point is that equality and innovation are complementary, not in tension: sectoral bargaining compresses wages across the economy, which both reduces inequality and forces more creative destruction, because firms that cannot compete on cheap labor have to innovate instead. The American problem is not a failure to invent. It is that the gains flow to the rich. The question AI raises is not whether the technology is real but who owns it and who captures its returns.

And here is the inversion Zitron gets backwards

The doomer instinct, shared by the Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez data-center moratorium, is to stop AI: pause the build, choke the data centers. In a June 2026 exchange with Matt Stoller, Bruenig points out that a pause is exactly what the incumbents want. A development pause lets them book counterfactual R&D as profit. A data-center pause lets them avoid the capex and charge more for a constrained token supply. The cartel move is to slow down. "The best way to stick it to them is to push them forward and socialize the equity." Use the competition that is forcing the labs to burn every dollar into capex, then publicly own the result.

That reframes the fact Zitron leans on hardest. "None of them are profitable" reads to him as proof the product is worthless. Read through the political economy, the unprofitability is competition forcing the labs to plow everything into building, which is the opposite of a monopoly extracting rent, and exactly what a public stake would capture. It is why Sanders' other bill, the American AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act, takes equity rather than banning the build: a public fund, board seats, and a dividend to ordinary Americans.

So the serious left ends up at the opposite of Zitron on every axis. The tech is real, not a con. The unprofitability is reinvestment, not worthlessness. The danger is distribution, not the technology. And the strategy is build and socialize, not pause and moralize.

A pause is the cartel's move. The way to beat the AI oligarchs is to push the technology forward and take the equity.

This is the throughline of the whole audit, stated in political-economy terms. Zitron gathers real energy by being angry at real problems, the power of the labs, the treatment of workers, the arrogance of the founders. Then he points that energy at the technology and predicts its collapse. The people who share his politics but keep the layers separate, Bruenig and the People's Policy Project, point the same energy at ownership and distribution, which is the target that can actually be hit. "It is a fraud and it will collapse" is not just wrong on the facts. It aims the fight in the one direction that helps the people he wants to beat.

Reliability and the Human Baseline

Hallucination is Zitron's favorite reliability club, and it deserves the deepest look on this page, because the strongest argument against him is also the one he half-relies on. The question "is hallucination inevitable" is really two questions that get conflated. The worst-case theory papers (Xu et al., Banerjee et al.) prove by diagonalization that no computable model can be correct on every computable function, so a fully general problem-solver must hallucinate somewhere. But those authors concede the proofs say nothing about the rate on real tasks. The practical literature, including OpenAI's own Why Language Models Hallucinate, Suzuki et al., and the Oracle Escape result, argues the rate is a fixable incentive and grounding problem. "Inevitable in the worst case" and "negligible in the average case" are both true. Zitron leans on the first to imply the second is impossible. That is the equivocation, and the authority he would most want, OpenAI's paper, is a reducible argument he would have to misread to cite.

Reliability in 2026 is bimodal, and that is the fact his framing flattens. Unsupervised on the open web it is genuinely bad: the CJR Tow Center found eight AI search engines wrong on more than 60 percent of news-citation queries, the BBC and EBU found 45 percent of responses had a significant issue, and Stanford's RegLab found commercial legal AI hallucinating 17 to 33 percent despite "hallucination-free" marketing, with 700-plus court cases of fabricated citations now on record. Grounded and verifiable, it is genuinely good: SimpleQA accuracy jumps from under 40 percent to over 90 percent with tools, and the best grounded systems sit under 1.5 percent hallucination. "Unreliable" is true for one mode and false for the other. He treats the bad mode as the whole.

Your reframe is the right correction, and the research backs it. The deployment standard is "better than the human you would otherwise use, faster and cheaper," not perfect. The human gold standard was always a myth: Loftus showed human memory confabulates confidently, so non-fabrication was never the bar we held people to. And we hold AI to a superhuman one: in one radiology study people tolerated an 11.3 percent error rate from a human and only 6.8 percent from the machine. Karpathy's reframe sharpens it: generation is the mechanism, a model is always dreaming, so the fix is grounding and system design, not waiting for a perfect oracle. Willison adds that a code hallucination is the least dangerous kind, because you run the code and the error surfaces. The augmentation evidence closes it: the BCG-Harvard field experiment found 40 percent higher quality inside the AI frontier, and the support-agent trial found gains of 14 percent overall and 34 percent for novices. A technology with those effects is not useless.

But the human analogy has a real limit, and it is exactly where Zitron is strongest, so honesty requires stating it. AI error is shaped differently from human error. It arrives in confident, fluent prose with no tells, where a person would hedge. It is systematic and correlated across millions of identical outputs, so one failure mode propagates at scale instead of being idiosyncratic and self-limiting. And it is indifferent to truth rather than mistaken about it. Add automation bias, where humans rubber-stamp the machine, deskilling, where a clinician's unassisted detection rate fell after AI exposure, and the accountability gap, and "human in the loop" is often a fiction that launders machine error. On the unsupervised high-stakes case, legal citations, medical recall, news, he wins cleanly, and vendor promises that the problem is solved are false.

One more nuance you raised, and it is the right one. Reliability is jagged. AI improves fastest where the output is verifiable, code and math, because a checkable reward can train it, and it lags where quality is subjective, writing and taste, where no such signal exists. So "soulless slop" has more standing for prose than for code, and "it cannot really code" has less. The honest map is not useless and not magic but a jagged frontier.

Hallucination is real, bimodal, and not going to zero. But "it hallucinates, therefore it is useless" is a category error the very literature he invokes refutes.

Does this move the rating? No, and the reason is the honest one. It makes his dismissal look worse, because his strongest-sounding move, citing inevitability, is an equivocation, and the comparative standard that actually governs deployment is one he never applies. But it also hands him his best ammunition, the error-shape disanalogy, which is real. A D-tier analyst who is loud about a genuine problem is still pointing at a genuine problem. His D is not for being wrong that hallucination exists. It is for the method: selecting the worst cases, misreading his own sources, and freezing a moving target. The topic is legitimate. The reasoning is what fails.
