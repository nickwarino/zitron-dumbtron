# Matt Bruenig on LLMs, Labor, and Dynamism

**Source:** The Bruenig's Podcast
**Date:** February 15, 2026
**Speakers:** Matt Bruenig, Liz (Bruenig)

Used in *Zitron Dumbtron* for analysis and criticism. This is the S-tier political-economy read in the speaker's own words: the technology is the wonder workers made, and the fight is over who owns it.

---

**Liz:** Do you have any qualms about using and promoting AI given the potential negative effects growth of this technology will have on workers?

**Matt:** I mean, I don't have any qualms using it. Like, what's that got to do with whether anyone else is going to use it, you know? Um... I don't know if I would say I promote them. I show people what I'm doing with them. But, um... No, I mean, look, it's like any other labor-saving technology that has come across again and again and again, right?

Word processing software completely wiped out typists. That used to be a whole job. There used to be tens of thousands of people employed as typists, and now there are none who are employed as typists. And you can go on and on down the line. Now, maybe this will be a significantly bigger disruption or whatever, but like... this is literally how all innovation and growth works. It's: You come up with a new way to do something that allows you to produce more stuff with less human labor. That could be replacing labor with machines and electricity—which is a lot of what the Industrial Revolution was, right? We take energy power and we combine it with machines and we use it to do farming, and we use it to produce textiles, and we use it to produce cars or whatever.

Or it could be productive technology like the assembly line—just kind of making it more efficient, the usage of labor more efficient. Or materials advances like fertilizer, which means that you can grow more food on the same plot of land with the same amount of labor.

At the end of the day, if you're going to get more production per person, you gotta find a way to replace labor with non-labor inputs. That's how it all works.

Now, ideally, you have a system set up where people who become unemployed... it's not a big deal if you become unemployed. Right? So, what if when you become unemployed you get 90% of your income from an unemployment benefit and you have healthcare taken care of? And you can, you know, chill until you can find a new job. It could take you a year, who knows? But you're covered, and it's not financially devastating. That's the situation essentially in Denmark, as I've described it. They've kind of got the best unemployment system.

But, you know, that's a failure of the economic system. That's a failure of the welfare system to not take care of people when they're moving from one job to another. And that happens all the time, by the way. Every month, if you look at the data, it's somewhere close to like 2 million people are losing their job. Depending on the month and the situation. Every month! If you look at the labor flows data. So that's 2 million... that means every year there's like 20-plus million episodes of people going from employment to unemployment. Every year.

So like, this chaos of people transitioning from employment to unemployment and then having to transition back into employment—that little three-step... those job transitions are occurring tens of millions of times a year. And we should have protection for people in that setting already. It should already be the case that when that happens, you're taken care of until you can get into a new job.

I don't really see anything different about this from that. Now, either we're going to do it or we're not, but... you know, I wish the welfare state was better. I think it's a really sad state of affairs... I'll go on a little bit here. When it's so easy to make sure that everyone is taken care of. That the gains from innovation flow to everyone. Right? We have levers that we can pull. Whether that's the welfare state, whether that's taxation, whether that's public ownership, whether that's collective bargaining... there's these levers we can pull that can change the way that income is distributed to make sure that people have security when they're transitioning from jobs, make sure that income is more evenly distributed...

This is a solved thing. And it's kind of silly that we are always confronted with that problem. Like, everything that happens... "Oh this is going to be bad because..." And it's like, well yes, because the system is bad. LLMs will be bad? Yep. Covid will be... mhm. Automating the ports is going to... mhm, yeah, sure is. Or, "What if consumer demand changes real quickly and people no longer want this product that used to... a lot of people were producing?" Like this was a product that employed millions of people in factories... but now consumers just don't really want it, they moved on to buying other stuff. "Well, that's going to be bad." Yeah.

It's like... but this is all the time! What you're describing is just dynamism. If you have a dynamic economy where things are being changed and reallocated all the time, then you're going to have transitions and disruptions. And there's easy systems to make sure that people's lives are not destroyed by them. And we just don't use them. And, you know, I don't know what you want to do about that. Are you going to be like, "So we'll just stop all dynamism! No dynamism in the economy! We'll stop that!" And then that will kind of fix the problem since we don't do the thing that accommodates dynamism in the welfare state? Like... good luck. You know? Good luck.

This is true with LLMs as well. Like, you're not going to... I have an LLM on my web server. Like, you can't delete them. I got one. Anyone could download them. There's millions of computers in the world that have LLMs on them. I'm not saying you have a subscription to an LLM service, I mean like, they just got them. You can't stop LLMs. It's a program. It's a piece of software. It's like a music file or something.

The last thing I'll say about this—and this is a much more general point—but I was thinking about this the other day. What is that verse? Solidarity Forever?

**Liz:** For the union makes us strong.

**Matt:** There's such a lovely verse in there that reminds me of all sorts of stuff you see in socialist writing where they say:

These are workers.

Like it was actually Mark Andreessen and Sam Altman, they made the LLM. And now Capital is... It's like, workers made this. This is an innovation of human beings. The working class has made, in a sense. People produced that.
