# Adam Tooze on What AI Means for the Economy and for Politics

**Source:** Ones and Tooze (Foreign Policy podcast)
**URL:** https://foreignpolicy.com/podcasts/ones-and-tooze/what-ai-means-for-the-economy-and-for-politics/
**Speakers:** Adam Tooze, host (Kat)

Used in *Zitron Dumbtron* for analysis and criticism. Tooze is half the standard the rating invokes (a Levine/Tooze read). This episode is his actual analysis of AI as a technology and a political economy. The jump-off data point: US tech firms will spend roughly $400 billion this year on AI infrastructure, projected to reach or exceed $3 trillion by 2028, possibly fueling nearly half of this year's estimated GDP growth.

---

**Host:** A phrase that comes up is "general purpose technology." What would that mean for the economy if AI were in fact a general purpose technology, and what are the precedents?

**Tooze:** This is a great place to start, because it takes us to the heart of how economists are trying to figure out how AI relates to the economy and to the familiar history of technological change. General purpose technology is a phrase economists coined to describe a puzzle: technologies that are conspicuous, epoch-defining, very pervasive, but in their immediate impact on the economy sometimes subtle, hard to detect, and yet over time become indispensable and almost impossible to imagine the world without. Steam power, electrification, the internal combustion engine, semiconductors. The idea is that AI might be that kind of technology.

Other innovations, iron-hulled ships, flight, space travel, are not on that list. Not because they don't matter, but because their impact is much more direct: they change or create a single industry. That is not a general purpose technology. The list is short, and what is striking about the AI debate is there isn't very much disagreement. Practically everyone agrees AI clearly is of the type of general purpose technologies. It is changing things dramatically, it is pervasive, and it may take time for its full effects to be felt, because what is typical of general purpose technologies is that it takes time to build the infrastructures and shift the societal arrangements to make full use of it. It is a long way from the first microchip to the entire logistical system built around Amazon, but in due course one enables the other.

The question with AI is not whether it is a general purpose technology, but whether it is something more than a normal one. Is it something hyper? Is it in some sense the endpoint of all technological development, because it is a technology about technology? What we are doing is applying technology to thinking, which is the source of technology.

**Tooze:** Standard neoclassical growth models do not actually predict sustained growth. They predict convergence to some level of GDP after which growth subsides. Technology figures as the factor that lets capital and labor be increasingly productive: total factor productivity. The real kicker is that if AI turns out to be an R&D-enhancing innovation, like the invention of the big lab, then we might see not just a one-off bump in growth, which is what general purpose technologies delivered in the past, but a quantum leap, because we can think faster, so we can innovate faster. Rather than diminishing returns in research, the biggest nightmare of conventional economic thinking, the low-hanging fruit being exhausted, the Gordon thesis, we would be in an era where AI lets us increase the pace of innovation progressively. People talk about sustained growth rates of 20 percent a year. We are not just getting smarter, we are getting smarter at getting smarter. That is what the debate is really about: is AI a normal general purpose technology, or this other type of break in historical development?

**Host:** Is the trajectory of AI improvement itself changing? Are we already seeing diminishing marginal returns?

**Tooze:** This is a hot item of debate within the AI community, and as a novice reading my way in I am struck by its intensity. The question is whether the scaling laws apply or whether we hit a wall. The scaling laws came from a 2020 OpenAI and DeepMind paper showing model performance improves smoothly with more data, more parameters, and more compute. The debate is not just about the laws as descriptions, but as management principles. If you are OpenAI, is the smart thing simply to scale up those three inputs? And if you optimize, do you still hit a wall? Very senior people, like Meta's chief AI officer, are convinced that on the current configuration there are constraints: it is fundamentally text-based, you are not retaining enough data as memory, you are not building up enough experience inside the models, and ramping up compute will not break those constraints. So an academic question becomes a management question, and it informs corporate strategy: Meta diversifies the data it feeds the models, and tries to shift them from predicting the most likely next token toward constructing models of the world, a kind of weltbild mirrored within the AI, so they can move to more grounded predictions and interactions. These are not abstract academic conversations. They are shaping billions of dollars of investment. And we saw the shock at the start of this year of DeepSeek emerging with a powerful model on much more modest compute. Everything to play for.

**Host:** Is AI an extractive technology, in the micro sense of environmental damage and the macro sense of centralizing power and reproducing inequality?

**Tooze:** I am not sure, honestly. I appreciate the polemical force, extractive industries are contentious, there is something violent inherent in extraction. But it is difficult to see why that makes AI distinctive. It is a thing in the world. Kate Crawford's line is that AI is neither artificial nor intelligent, it is made from natural resources and human labor. We will need a lot of energy and water for cooling and land for these facilities. The two aspects worth digging into are that it reminds us labor is involved, and the intellectual property question.

People think of AI as displacing labor, but the mind-boggling fact is that none of these models would work the way they do unless they were anchored in what the industry calls ground truth, a phrase from digital geography, a data point that some person has been on the ground to confirm. The human-like quality of the models depends on massive human coding of text, cat, cat, animal, associated with an image. The big firms have employed millions of people, a large fraction of the online transient digital service workforce, anchoring the AI's manipulation of symbols in crude, human-approved chains. And when we get to alignment, deciding that a thought is problematic, that is a human input, content moderation that is mind-bogglingly crude epistemologically and politically, because that is where the normative political work is going on, and many of these judgments are culturally specific. There is a huge amount of underpaid hidden human labor.

The other thing being used is huge volumes of text, in what is surely one of the most gigantic acts of, they would insist, fair use, but a series of lawsuits against Meta and Apple and others allege the intellectual property of Reuters, the Encyclopedia Britannica, and Disney has been plundered. There is the nightmarish vision of one firm buying millions of books and literally stripping the pages out to churn through scanners, which is in the Anthropic case. With Google Books, serious copyright issues were understood as a major struggle over IP. The AI firms a decade later just barreled in and are now waging the legal fight case by case, and so far winning, because the judges are impressed by the creative use and label it fair use. In some cases you are really treating a book as though it were a raw material. That is the most obvious element where we are really talking about extraction.

**Host:** Henry Farrell describes AI as a cultural and social technology, splitting the difference between the apocalyptic and the normal-technology visions. Do you find that plausible?

**Tooze:** This is exactly the right approach. Henry is one of the wise people in the social science field. His point, in that piece in Science, is to take all the silly sci-fi anthropomorphic nonsense off the table. This is not a human being and this is not human intelligence. What it clearly is, is an amazing statistical indexing, search, generative, symbol-generating mechanism. It is a bit like synthesizer music. We have not had one this powerful before, but we have had symbol-generating mechanisms before, and they are very powerful and they change societies and our relationship to language, like writing, like computers, like the printed book.

Take off the table the obscure metaphysical arguments about whether this is general intelligence or really human. Of course it isn't. But can a novel induce feelings in you? Can you fall in love with a character in a TV series? Of course. So could AI, if it can sympathetically affirm things in you, spot patterns in your speech and render them back to you, induce different emotions in you? Of course it can. That is the scary thing about symbol systems, whether it is music that makes us cry or battle cries that take you over the top to your death cheering. These mechanisms work, and this is a very powerful engine for generating those effects. So all the questions are: what is it going to do to our politics, our social systems, to those of us who are scribes in the face of the printing press? How will we define creativity, more in the mode of curating, selecting, prompting? We are going to be like racing drivers manipulating a very fast machine, not runners. We do not yet have categories for it.

I keep remembering what it was like to first meet Google, and it changed my life. You can see it in my books, the one written before Google and the ones after. The previous search engine was a giant bound book in Cambridge, or the mechanized card catalogs in the Staatsbibliothek in Berlin. This technology lets us do so much more. It is an animated relational database. It is not diminishing to describe it as a technology on the level of print, or bureaucracy, or markets as inventions. We are not entertaining the apocalyptic scenarios, but these are still world-changing concepts.

**Host:** Could AI solve the socialist calculation debate and enable a truly planned economy?

**Tooze:** This goes back a hundred years, to the aftermath of World War One and the first Soviet system, the promise of replacing the anarchy of the market with the visible hand of planning. The Austrian school argued there was a rationalist fallacy: the sheer complexity of the system, the tacit knowledge that is in the doing not the telling, and the problem of formulating coherent social preferences, which always tempts an authoritarian answer. Who knows how to make a pencil? No one, and no one needs to, because the market intermediates. But this was a massively oversimplified argument on both sides. We never saw classic central planners with one big computer crunching numbers, and markets are not one organic magic solution either, there are huge varieties, from a microsecond high-frequency futures exchange to a flea market with no price labels. Both are markets.

And bureaucracy has always been present in markets, because large organizations have to make sensible bets. My university plans how many students it will get, we are central planners of our slice of a bureaucratized education market. Amazon is estimated to account for 13 percent of American retail spending through its algorithmically driven mechanisms, and online is around a quarter of US retail. All of that is amenable to algorithmic AI management. They do not need to know what is in my heart, they look at what I click and feed me tailored advertising. The entire system of free social media is a giant engine for avoiding the Hayek problem, feeding revealed preferences into algorithms that spit out tailored mechanisms. So the problem was falsely posed at the start. We already live in a world where large slices of our retail behavior are algorithmically controlled and catered to, and in China the share is even larger.

**Host:** Is the only plausible precedent the industrial revolution, on an accelerated scale? What should we keep in mind about work going forward?

**Tooze:** If we grant AI is a general purpose technology but insist it is so far meaningful to treat it as a normal one, you could go back to the steam engine, the 18th and 19th century industrial revolution in fossil fuels and the mechanization of factory work, which hit manual labor, textiles first. That is not what is going on now. The fundamental difference between the AI revolution and the industrial revolution is that this is touching all of the zones of mental and symbolic labor, which became more and more important in the modern economy. The late 19th century bureaucratic revolution, Alfred Chandler's world of the modern corporation, big government, lawyering, social services, white-collar office work. That is what this technology is going to impact most. It is a general purpose symbolic cultural technology, to pick up Farrell's point, that is going to revise the mechanisms of office and white-collar work that have been central to modern economies.

So the question is, is this like the telegraph, electricity, the computer, an organic development of the symbol-shuffling we have seen since the second industrial revolution, or is this like the invention of the printing press, after which the Renaissance, the industrial revolution, and everything else came within the frame it set? And could the conversation about the wall push a firm like Meta to escape it by moving into a technology that encounters the physical world directly, escaping the office and the laptop, going out into the world and becoming even more pervasive and even more general?
